Foundations 0 · the deepest “why”

Why do numbers exist at all? From counting to j

Every kind of number was invented because a real job could not be done with the numbers people already had. Complex numbers are not magic: they are the fifth time this happened. Walk up the ladder and you will see why electricians need them.

1 · A number is a tool, not a thing you find in nature

Nobody has ever seen “3” lying on the ground. You have seen three sheep. A number is a tool we made to answer a question. The first question was “how many?”, so we made the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, … Whenever the tool failed on a real job, people did not give up. They extended the tool. This happened five times, and the fifth time gave us j.

2 · Rung 1: counting numbers. Failure: take away more than you have

Counting numbers can add and multiply forever and always stay inside the family. But ask “3 − 5” and there is no answer. You cannot have −2 sheep. Yet merchants really did owe more than they had. Fix: invent zero and negative numbers. The honest meaning of a negative number is not “less than nothing”. It is “the same size, in the opposite direction”: 5 m east and −5 m (5 m west), a deposit and a debt, a current flowing forward and a current flowing back. This is the first time a number carries a size and a direction. Remember that, it is the whole plot.

3 · Rung 2: whole numbers. Failure: share one thing between three

“1 ÷ 3” has no whole-number answer. Yet people really needed to split a loaf or a field. Fix: fractions (1/3, 2/5 …). Every number now sits between two whole numbers, and any two fractions can be added, subtracted, multiplied and divided (except by zero).

4 · Rung 3: fractions. Failure: the diagonal of a square

Draw a square with sides of 1 m. By Pythagoras the diagonal is √1² + 1² = √2 m. That length obviously exists, you can cut a string that long, but it is not any fraction (the Greeks proved this and it shocked them). Fix: irrational numbers. Fractions plus these new ones fill the number line with no gaps. We call this complete line the real numbers. “Real” here only means “a point on the line”.

5 · Rung 4: the real line. Failure: a number that, multiplied by itself, gives −1

Positive × positive is positive. Negative × negative is also positive. So no real number squared gives −1. For a long time that simply meant “this equation has no answer”. Then in 1545 Cardano tried to solve cubic equations such as x³ = 15x + 4. The answer 4 is obviously right (4³ = 64 = 60 + 4). But his formula only reached it by passing through √−121. If he pretended √−1 behaved like a normal symbol, the impossible parts cancelled and the correct real answer 4 came out. A trick that gives right answers is a tool worth keeping. Fix: invent j with j² = −1.

6 · But what IS j? It is a turn

For 250 years j was called “imaginary” and treated as a trick. Then Wessel, Argand and Gauss noticed a picture. On the number line, multiplying by −1 flips a number to the other side: a 180° turn. If two identical turns make a 180° flip, one of them must be a 90° turn. Where does a 90° turn point? Not along the line. It needs a second direction at right angles: up. So we draw a second axis, and call a 90° turn j. Twice: j × j = −1. That is all. j is not “the square root of a negative” in some mysterious sense. It is “turn a quarter turn”.

7 · Why this matters for electricity

AC current does not only have a size. It also has timing (phase), the angle it has turned through its cycle. Size plus angle needs two numbers, exactly like a point on a plane. A complex number is precisely that: one object holding a size and an angle. So AC maths with complex numbers is the same ladder idea again: our tool (real numbers, size only) failed on a real job (waves that are shifted in time), so we extend it.

The pattern to remember each new kind of number added one more freedom. Counting: how many. Negatives: which way (two ways). Fractions: how much between. Irrationals: no gaps. Complex: any direction. That last freedom is what rotation, and therefore AC, needs.

Foundations 1

What is electricity, really? Following one electron

Before volts and amps, here is what is physically happening inside a copper wire. Once you can picture this, DC and AC stop being formulas.

1 · Everything is made of atoms, and atoms contain charge

An atom has a heavy nucleus with positive charge (protons) and light electrons with negative charge orbiting it. Normally the charges cancel, so the atom is neutral. Charge is measured in coulombs (C). One electron carries only 0.000 000 000 000 000 000 16 C (1.6 × 10−19 C). That is why a coulomb is a huge crowd: about 6 240 000 000 000 000 000 electrons (6.24 × 1018).

2 · In metals some electrons are free

In copper, each atom lets go of about one outer electron. The atoms (now positive ions) sit fixed in a regular lattice. The released electrons wander between them like a gas. These are the free electrons. In an insulator (rubber, glass, air) the electrons are held tightly and almost none are free. That single difference is why copper carries current and rubber does not.

3 · With no push, free electrons move randomly

At room temperature the free electrons zip around at roughly 1 000 000 m/s, but in every direction, bouncing off the ions. At any instant as many go left as right, so nothing flows overall. No current.

4 · Voltage is the push

Connect a battery across the wire. Its voltage creates an electric field along the wire. The field pushes every electron toward the positive end. The electrons keep colliding, but now each collision restarts a tiny bit of steady drift in the same direction. The average sideways motion is called the drift velocity.

Voltage V (volts)The push, or the energy given to each coulomb. 1 V = 1 joule per coulomb.
Current I (amps)How many coulombs pass a point each second. 1 A = 1 C/s ≈ 6.24 × 1018 electrons per second.
Resistance R (ohms)How much the lattice gets in the way: more collisions, less flow for the same push.
Power P (watts)Energy per second. The energy lost in collisions becomes heat.

5 · The surprise: electrons are slow

Drift speed is v = I ÷ (n · A · q), where n is free electrons per m³ (8.5 × 1028 in copper), A is wire cross-section and q = 1.6 × 10−19 C. For 1 A in a 1 mm² wire that is 1 ÷ (8.5×1028 × 10−6 × 1.6×10−19) = 0.000 073 5 m/s: 0.07 mm per second. A snail is faster. The lamp lights instantly because the field travels at nearly the speed of light and pushes all the electrons at once, like a pipe already full of water.

6 · What changes with AC

With DC the push always points the same way, so electrons drift one way, continuously. With AC the push reverses 100 times a second (at 50 Hz). Each electron just wiggles back and forth around the same spot and never goes anywhere. For 1 A rms in 1 mm² the wiggle is smaller than a thousandth of a millimetre (about 0.33 µm). Yet the lamp still heats and lights, because heat comes from collisions, and collisions happen whichever way the electron moves. That is also why RMS is the right number for AC: heat depends on the square of the current, and the square of a negative number is positive. (RMS has its own chapter below.)

Power travels in the field, not in the electronsThe electrons are only the “pipe contents”. The energy flows through the field around the wire. In AC the electrons barely move, and power still arrives at your house.

More pressure gives more flow. A narrower pipe gives less flow. Written down:

I = VR   ·   V = I × R   ·   R = VI   (Ohm’s law)

Cover-the-unknown triangle: put V on top, I and R at the bottom. Cover what you want. Cover V and you see I × R. Cover I and you see V over R. Try it in the lab.

Power: how fast energy is turned into heat

Each coulomb loses V joules; I coulombs pass every second; so P = V × I. Using Ohm’s law you get two more forms, and you will need all three:

P = V × I = I² × R = V²R   (watts)

Notice I² and V². Double the current and the heat goes four times up. This square is the whole reason for RMS later.

Worked example

A 12 V battery drives a 40 Ω heater. I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3 A P = V × I = 12 × 0.3 = 3.6 W Check: I² R = 0.09 × 40 = 3.6 W ✓ V² ÷ R = 144 ÷ 40 = 3.6 W ✓

Foundations 3

Series circuits and the voltage divider

One road, many toll gates. The same traffic goes through all of them.

What “series” means

Parts are in series when the current has only one path through them, one after the other. Break it anywhere and everything stops (that is why an open in a series circuit kills the whole thing, Module 4).

CurrentThe same in every part. Electrons cannot pile up or vanish, so what enters one resistor must leave it and enter the next.
VoltagesThe drops add up to the source: VS = V1 + V2 + V3 (Kirchhoff’s voltage law: energy given = energy used).
ResistanceResistances add: RT = R1 + R2 + R3. More gates, more hindrance.

The voltage divider (very important)

Because the current is shared, each resistor takes a slice of the voltage in proportion to its resistance:

Vn = VS × RnRT

A bigger resistor takes a bigger share. This one rule becomes the AC voltage divider (filters, capacitor/inductor drops) in Module 3.

The DC equivalent

If you only care about what the battery sees, the whole chain acts like one resistor RT. Replace the chain, draw the same current: nothing outside can tell the difference. This equivalent idea is how every circuit is simplified, and in AC the single resistor becomes a single impedance Z.

Example: 12 V across 100 Ω, 200 Ω and 300 Ω in series RT = 100 + 200 + 300 = 600 Ω I = 12 ÷ 600 = 0.02 A = 20 mA (same in all three) V1 = 20 mA × 100 = 2 V V2 = 4 V V3 = 6 V sum = 12 V ✓ Divider check: V3 = 12 × 300 ÷ 600 = 6 V ✓ Power: 12 V × 20 mA = 0.24 W = 0.04 + 0.08 + 0.12 ✓

Foundations 4

Parallel circuits and the current divider

Many roads between the same two towns. Same height drop for all; traffic splits.

What “parallel” means

Parts are in parallel when both ends connect to the same two points. House sockets are in parallel: each appliance gets full mains voltage, and switching one off does not affect the others.

VoltageThe same across every branch (they share the same two nodes).
CurrentsThe branch currents add up to the total: IT = I1 + I2 + I3 (Kirchhoff’s current law: what flows in must flow out).
ResistanceThe total is smaller than the smallest branch. Each new road makes traffic easier.

Combining parallel resistors

Think in conductance G = 1/R (siemens). Conductances add, because each branch offers extra flow:

1RT = 1R1 + 1R2 + 1R3   ·   two resistors: RT = R1 × R2R1 + R2   ·   n equal ones: Rn

The current divider

The current prefers the easy road: each branch takes a share proportional to its conductance, so a smaller resistor takes a bigger current. For two branches:

I1 = IT × R2R1 + R2   ·   I2 = IT × R1R1 + R2

Note the swap: the other resistor goes on top. General form for any branch: In = IT × RT ÷ Rn. In AC this becomes “admittance divider”, and the parallel formula you met in the notes (1/Z) is the same thing with j.

Example: 12 V across 60 Ω and 40 Ω in parallel Each branch has 12 V: I1 = 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2 A I2 = 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3 A IT = 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.5 A RT = (60 × 40) ÷ (60 + 40) = 24 Ω check: 12 ÷ 24 = 0.5 A ✓ Divider: I1 = 0.5 × 40 ÷ 100 = 0.2 A ✓ (the smaller 40 Ω takes more)

Foundations 5

Series-parallel: reduce, solve, expand

Real circuits mix both. The method never changes.

The three-move method

Reduce. Replace any parallel group by its equivalent, then any series chain by its sum. Repeat until one resistor RT remains. Each time you replace, you are drawing the DC equivalent.
Solve at the top. IT = VS ÷ RT.
Expand back. Go backwards through your drawings. A series part gets V = IT × R. A parallel group gets its voltage from that, then each branch current is V ÷ R (or use the current divider).

Practise on R1 in series with (R2 parallel R3) in series with R4 in the lab. It writes out every step.

This is exactly how you will do ACReplace every R by a complex impedance Z, and every “add” by a complex add. The three moves are identical.

Foundations 6

Vectors, angles, and where the sine wave comes from

An arrow with a length and a direction. That is all a vector is, and it is the key to phasors.

Scalars and vectors

A scalar is just a size: 5 kg, 20 °C, 230 V. A vector has a size and a direction: “walk 5 m north-east”. We draw it as an arrow: the length is the size, the arrow points the direction.

Adding vectors: head to tail

Walk 3 m east, then 4 m north. You end up 5 m away, not 7 m, because the legs are at 90°. Draw the first arrow, start the second at its head, and the sum goes from the very start to the very end. If the arrows point the same way the sizes add; if opposite they subtract; at other angles the answer is in between.

|A + B| = √A² + B² + 2·A·B·cos θ    (θ = angle between them)

At θ = 90° the last term vanishes and you get Pythagoras: √3² + 4² = 5. This is why 3 A + 4 A can equal 5 A in AC circuits. It is not an error: the currents are at 90° (resistor branch and inductor branch).

Components: splitting an arrow into east and north parts

Any arrow of length r at angle θ has a horizontal part x = r cos θ and a vertical part y = r sin θ. Going back: r = √x² + y² and θ = tan−1(y ÷ x). Adding vectors is easiest in components: add all the x’s, add all the y’s.

Sine and cosine from a circle

Take an arrow of length 1 and spin it anticlockwise. At angle θ its tip is at (cos θ, sin θ). So sin θ is the height of the tip and cos θ is its distance across. Plot that height against the angle as the arrow spins and the curve you draw is the sine wave. This is the entire secret of AC: a generator coil spinning in a field produces exactly this height. Every AC voltage is the height of a spinning arrow.

Degrees and radians

One full turn is 360° = 2π radians (2π ≈ 6.283). So π rad = 180°, π/2 = 90°, 1 rad ≈ 57.3°. If the arrow makes f turns per second, its angle changes by 2πf radians per second. That rate is the angular frequency ω = 2πf, and the angle at time t is θ = ωt. At 50 Hz, ω = 314 rad/s.

Two AC voltages of the same frequency but different timing (phase) can’t be added as plain numbers: their peaks don’t line up. Draw each as a spinning arrow and add the arrows. A phasor is just that arrow frozen at one instant.

Foundations 7

Complex numbers for absolute beginners

Complex numbers are not complicated. They are a way to do vector maths with ordinary algebra. We build the whole thing from nothing.

1 · The number line, and a number nobody could find

Positive numbers sit to the right of zero, negatives to the left. Multiplying by −1 flips a number to the other side: 3 becomes −3. Flipping is a turn of 180° about zero. Now ask: what number multiplied by itself gives −1? Try 1 (gives 1) and −1 (gives 1). No number on the line works, because a number times itself is never negative.

2 · The key idea: half a flip

If ×(−1) is a 180° turn, there must be something that is a 90° turn: do it twice and you get 180°, which is ×(−1). We call this 90° turn j. So by definition: j × j = −1, or j = √−1. It was named “imaginary” as an insult, but it is just “turn 90° anticlockwise”. (Mathematicians write i. Engineers write j because i already means current.)

A 90° turn needs a second direction, so we add an up-and-down axis. Real numbers go along the horizontal axis. j-numbers go along the vertical one. Together they make the complex plane.

4 · A complex number is a point (or an arrow)

Write z = a + jb. a (the real part) is how far east, b (the imaginary part) is how far north. 3 + j4 is the point 3 east, 4 north: exactly the vector from before. This is rectangular form.

5 · The same arrow in polar form

The arrow also has a length |z| = r and an angle θ. Written r∠θ. Convert:

r = √a² + b²   ·   θ = tan−1(ba)   ·   a = r cos θ   ·   b = r sin θ
Quadrant traptan−1 on a calculator only answers between −90° and +90°. For −3 + j4 the calculator says −53.1°, but the point is up-left, so the real angle is 180° − 53.1° = 126.9°. Always sketch where the point is first.

6 · Arithmetic

  • Add / subtract: use rectangular. Add the real parts, add the imaginary parts. (3 + j4) + (1 − j2) = 4 + j2.
  • Multiply: in polar, multiply lengths, add angles. 5∠53° × 2∠−30° = 10∠23°. In rectangular, multiply like brackets and use j² = −1: (3 + j4)(1 − j2) = 3 − j6 + j4 − j²8 = 3 − j2 + 8 = 11 − j2.
  • Divide: in polar, divide lengths, subtract angles. In rectangular, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom (flip the sign of j): (3 + j4) ÷ (1 − j2) = (3 + j4)(1 + j2) ÷ 5 = (3 + j6 + j4 − 8) ÷ 5 = −1 + j2.

The conjugate trick works because (a + jb)(a − jb) = a² + b², a plain real number. No j left in the bottom.

7 · Why this helps AC

Multiplying by j turns an arrow 90°. A capacitor’s current is 90° ahead of its voltage; an inductor’s voltage is 90° ahead of its current. So reactances are j-numbers: ZL = jXL, ZC = −jXC. Ordinary Ohm’s law then works for AC with no trig at all: V = I × Z.

Foundations · the bridge to the real circuit

Phasor or complex number? What is the difference, and how one becomes the other

People use the two words as if they mean the same thing. They do not. A complex number is a kind of number. A phasor is a complex number that has been given a job: to stand for one sine wave. Once you see the five steps from a real wave on a scope to a phasor in an equation, AC maths stops being a bag of tricks.

1 · A complex number is just a number

A complex number is a + jb: an “across” part and an “up” part, or equally a length and an angle. It is the same kind of thing as 7 or 2/3, just with two parts. It does not know anything about electricity. Mathematicians use it for geometry, for rotations, for solving equations, and engineers use it for many different electrical things.

2 · A phasor is a complex number with a job

A phasor is a complex number used to stand for one sine wave at one fixed frequency. Its length is the size of the wave (peak or RMS). Its angle is the starting angle (phase) of the wave. That is all it stores. The frequency is not stored, because in an AC circuit it is the same everywhere and is written once, on the supply.

The one-line ruleEvery phasor is a complex number. Not every complex number is a phasor.

3 · Complex numbers in AC that are NOT phasors

Impedance Z = R + jX. It is complex, but it does not stand for a wave. It is a ratio V ÷ I, and its angle is the difference in phase between voltage and current. It never spins, not even in imagination. Admittance Y is the same. Complex power S = P + jQ is complex, but it does not oscillate at the supply frequency either: P is a steady average and Q is a size of swing. And the symbol j itself is a complex number that is only a rotation instruction.

4 · What you can do with each

With plain complex numbers you can do anything: add, subtract, multiply, divide. With phasors you can add and subtract any two of the same frequency, because that is adding the two waves. But multiplying two phasors does not give a phasor. Volts times amps makes a power wave that swings at twice the frequency, so a phasor cannot describe it. Multiplying a phasor by an impedance does give a phasor: V = I × Z, because Z is not a wave, it is only a “scale and turn” instruction.

5 · The bridge, in five steps

The real wave. A scope shows v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ): a size, a speed and a start angle.
Spin it. Picture an arrow of length Vm, starting at angle φ, spinning anticlockwise at ω. As a complex number that arrow is Vmej(ωt+φ).
Take the shadow. The height of the arrow tip is exactly Vm sin(ωt + φ). So the real wave is the up-part (imaginary part) of the spinning complex number.
Freeze it. The spin ejωt is common to every voltage and current in a linear circuit, so take it out of every equation. What is left, Vmejφ = Vm∠φ, is the phasor.
Do algebra, then go back. Add arrows, use V = IZ, divide, whatever you need. To return to a real wave, multiply the answer by ejωt and take the height.

6 · Why we are allowed to drop the spin

In a circuit made of resistors, coils and capacitors, if you push a sine wave in, a sine wave of the same frequency comes out. Only the size and the timing change. So all the waves in the circuit are spinning arrows turning at the same speed. If every arrow turns together, you can ride on a spinning platform where all of them look still, and only the angles between them matter. That is the phasor diagram.

Foundations 8

RMS, average and the DC equivalent: the full story

One question drives everything here: “how much DC would do the same job as this AC?”

The problem

An AC wave changes every instant: 0, 100, 300, 100, 0, −100… Which single number tells you how strong it is? Peak is the highest it ever gets, but it only touches that for a moment. Average over a full cycle is zero, which is useless. We need a number that reflects work done.

The idea: compare with DC by heat

A DC supply gives steady heat P = V²/R. Define the AC’s effective value as the DC voltage that heats the same resistor equally. Heat depends on V², and squaring removes the minus signs, so negative half-cycles also heat. That gives a recipe, and the recipe has a name made from its own steps, read backwards:

S — Square every instantaneous value (heat ∝ v²). All values become positive.
M — Mean: average those squares over one cycle. That is the average heat.
R — Root: take the square root to get back from “volts squared” to volts.

Read it backwards: Root of the Mean of the Squares = RMS.

Why exactly ½?

Since sin²θ = ½ − ½ cos 2θ, and the cos 2θ part swings equally above and below zero, its average is zero. What remains is ½. Therefore the mean of sin² is ½ and the root is 1/√2 = 0.707. For other shapes the number differs (table below).

Mains example

“230 V” mains is the RMS value. Its peak is 230 × 1.414 = 325 V. A 230 V rms supply across a 46 Ω heater gives Irms = 5 A and P = 230 × 5 = 1150 W, exactly what 230 V DC would give. Meters and the notes always quote RMS unless they say peak.

Why do aircraft use AC at all?

Direct current is simple. So why do large aircraft, factories and your home all run on alternating current?

Where AC is used

Alternating current (AC) systems are found on most multi-engine, high-performance, turbine-powered and transport-category aircraft. It is the same electricity used in industry and in your home. Direct current (DC) is used on systems that must be compatible with battery power, such as light aircraft and automobiles.

Benefits of AC over DC

  • Transmission. AC can be sent over long distances more readily and more economically, because AC voltage can be raised or lowered by transformers.
  • Space and weight. AC devices, especially motors, are smaller and simpler than DC devices. As more units are operated electrically, the saving grows, especially on large transport aircraft.
  • Maintenance. Most AC motors need no brushes, so they need less maintenance than DC motors.
  • Circuit breakers. They work satisfactorily under load at high altitude on AC. On DC, arcing is so severe that breakers must be replaced often.
  • 400-cycle equipment. Most aeroplanes with a 24 V DC system have equipment that needs some 400-cycle AC. An inverter changes DC to AC for it.

Effective values are what meters show

Most AC meters display the effective value of the AC. Voltage and current ratings of a system or component are given in effective values. Peak and instantaneous values are used only in very limited situations and are stated as such. In the study of AC, any value given for current or voltage is assumed to be an effective value unless stated otherwise.

How a spinning loop makes AC

Before the vocabulary, see where the sine wave comes from. Drag the 3D scene to look at the alternator from any side.

Faraday's rule in one line

When a conductor cuts magnetic field lines, a voltage is induced in it. For a conductor of length l moving at speed v across a field B, the voltage is proportional to the part of the velocity that crosses the field lines.

e = B · l · v · sin θ

A loop turned at a steady speed has its two long sides moving in a circle. When the loop plane is square-on to the field (θ = 0°), the sides move along the field lines and cut none, so e = 0. A quarter turn later (θ = 90°) the sides cut straight across the field and e is at its peak. Keep turning and the sides cut the field the other way round, so the voltage reverses. That repeating swing is a sine wave.

e = Epeak · sin(ωt)    with   ω = 2πf

Cycles per revolution (notes p.5)

In a generator the voltage and current pass through a complete cycle each time a coil passes under a north and a south pole. The number of cycles for each revolution equals the number of pairs of poles. So frequency is cycles per revolution multiplied by revolutions per second.

f = (pole pairs) × (revolutions per second)
Aircraft numberA 4-pole-pair generator turned at 6000 rpm (100 rev/s) gives 4 × 100 = 400 Hz, the 400-cycle supply mentioned on page 4.

3.1.1 · Notes pp.5–8

Period, frequency, cycle and phase

Four words describe nearly every sine wave. Learn them as one family.

Three values of AC

Three values apply to both voltage and current and help define the sine wave: instantaneous, peak and effective. The text talks about voltage but the values apply to current in all AC circuits.

Period

The time for a sine wave to complete one full cycle is the period (P). It is measured in seconds, milliseconds or microseconds. Period is inversely proportional to frequency: the higher the frequency, the shorter the period.

P = 1f      f = 1P

Frequency

Frequency is the number of cycles per second (cps). The unit is the hertz (Hz). Figure 3-3 shows a wave at 2 cycles per second above a wave at 8 cycles per second. In a generator, frequency equals the cycles per revolution multiplied by the revolutions per second.

Cycle

A cycle is the completion of a pattern. Whenever a voltage or current goes through a series of changes, returns to its starting point and repeats, that series is a cycle. One complete AC cycle is 360°. It is usual to start where the voltage is zero. The voltage rises to a positive maximum, falls to zero, rises to a negative maximum and returns to zero.

There are two alternations in a complete cycle: positive and negative. The polarity reverses each half cycle, so electrons flow one way in the positive half cycle and the opposite way in the negative half.

Phase relationships

Phase is the relationship between two sine waves, measured in degrees. It can be measured between the voltages of two alternators, or between the current and voltage of the same alternator.

  • In phase (Fig 3-4A): both waves pass their maximum and minimum points at the same time and in the same direction. A difference of 360° is the same as in phase.
  • Out of phase: the waves reach maximum and minimum at different times. The one that reaches its maximum first leads; the other lags. The difference in degrees is stated (Fig 3-4B shows 90°).
  • 180° apart (Fig 3-4C): they are exactly opposite. When one is at its positive peak the other is at its negative peak.

3.1.2 · Notes pp.8–10

Instantaneous, peak, average, RMS and peak-to-peak

One wave, five numbers. The skill is knowing which number a meter, a rating or a scope is giving you.

Instantaneous

The value at any instant along the wave. Any point on the sine wave is an instantaneous value. It goes from zero at 0° to maximum at 90°, back to zero at 180°, to maximum in the opposite direction at 270° and to zero again at 360°.

v = Vpeak · sin θ

Peak

The largest instantaneous value, also called the maximum value. The largest positive value occurs at 90° and the largest negative one at 270°. Peak values are seldom used by aircraft technicians.

Peak-to-peak

The maximum in one direction to the maximum in the other direction. It is measured top to bottom and is often used on oscilloscopes. Most calculations need the peak value, so divide peak-to-peak by two.

Effective (RMS)

The effective value is always less than the peak. An AC circuit of 24 V and 2 A should produce the same heat in a resistor as a DC circuit of 24 V and 2 A. It is also called root mean square, from the way it is calculated.

Vrms = 0.707 × Vpeak     Vpeak = 1.41 × Vrms

So a 110 V AC supply has a peak of 110 × 1.41 ≈ 155 V.

3.2 · Notes pp.11–13

Square, triangular and sawtooth waves

Fourier's big idea: every repeating wave is a stack of sine waves. Build the shapes yourself.

Fourier and harmonics

Fourier (1768–1830), a French mathematician, was one of the first to realise that all periodic waves can be built up by combining sine waves of the right amplitude, frequency and phase. The sine wave with the lowest frequency is the fundamental, and the resulting wave has the same frequency as the fundamental. Harmonics are sine waves at two, three, four times and so on the fundamental frequency.

Square waves (Fig 3-8)

A perfect square wave has vertical sides and a flat top. In theory it has an infinite number of odd harmonics and no even harmonics. It cannot be made perfectly in electronic circuits, but the fundamental plus the odd harmonics from the 3rd to the 19th gives a good resemblance. Fewer harmonics make the sides slope.

A square wave is often used as a test signal at the input of a system:

  • If the system does not respond well to high frequencies, the sides slope.
  • If it does not respond well to low frequencies, the flat parts become curved.
  • If an amplifier fails with a square wave it is unlikely to work with other periodic waves.

Digital logic circuits use square-wave type signals at their gate inputs and outputs.

Triangular and sawtooth waves (Figs 3-9, 3-10)

A perfect sawtooth contains an infinite number of both odd and even harmonics. The lower harmonics shape the rising portion and the higher harmonics shape the decay time. The triangular wave rises at a linear rate from zero to a peak, falls linearly, then does the same with reversed polarity. The sawtooth climbs from its negative peak to its positive peak at a linear rate and then returns quickly. If the return takes a little time, the sawtooth looks more like a triangle.

Where complex numbers and phasors come from

The notes use a right-angled triangle for R, X and Z. This section shows why a triangle works, and why engineers call the sides "real" and "imaginary".

1 · A sine wave is a shadow of a spinning arrow

Look at a point going round a circle at a steady speed. Its height above the centre line is a sine wave in time. A voltage that swings as Vm cos(ωt + φ) can therefore be pictured as an arrow of length Vm, spinning at ω radians per second, starting at angle φ. The wave is the arrow's shadow.

2 · A number that rotates

Ordinary numbers live on a line. To describe an arrow you need two numbers: how far across and how far up. Engineers keep them together as a + jb. Define j as "turn 90° anticlockwise". Turning 90° twice points the arrow backwards, which is multiplication by −1. So j × j = −1, written j² = −1. That is all "imaginary" means: a sideways direction.

1545 · Cardano met square roots of negative numbers while solving cubic equations, and hated them.
1572 · Bombelli wrote the first rules for adding and multiplying them. They worked.
1637 · Descartes called them "imaginary", meant as an insult.
1748–1777 · Euler linked them to the sine and cosine, and introduced the letter i.
1797–1806 · Wessel, Argand, Gauss drew them as points on a plane: real across, imaginary up.
1893 · Steinmetz showed how complex numbers turn AC circuit problems into simple algebra. Electrical engineers write j because i already means current.

3 · Euler's formula links rotation and sine waves

ejθ = cos θ + j sin θ

The point ejθ sits on a circle of radius 1. Its real part is cos θ and its imaginary part is sin θ. Spin it with θ = ωt and the real part is a cosine wave. So a voltage can be written as the real part of a spinning arrow:

v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ) = Re{ Vmejφ · ejωt }

Every quantity in the circuit carries the same spin ejωt, so we leave it out and keep only Vmejφ = Vm∠φ. That stripped-down number is a phasor. Adding two voltages of the same frequency is adding two arrows, which is just adding their complex numbers.

4 · Why R, XL and XC are at right angles

A capacitor's current is i = C·dv/dt. The slope of a cosine is a sine pushed 90° ahead, and the slope also brings a factor ω. In complex form, differentiating means multiplying by jω. So:

Capacitor: I = jωC·V  →  V/I = 1jωC = −j·1ωC = −jXC
Inductor:  V = jωL·I  →  V/I = +jωL = +jXL
Resistor:   V = R·I   →  V/I = R

The j in front of X is the 90° turn. R is along the real axis, X along the imaginary axis, so the notes' impedance triangle is exactly the complex plane. Impedance is Z = R + jX and its size is Z = √(R² + X²), the Pythagorean theorem the notes use on page 14.

5 · Phasor, or just a complex number?

A phasor is a complex number that stands for a sinusoid at a known frequency. Impedance Z, admittance Y and complex power S are complex numbers too, but no waveform v(t) corresponds to them, so they are not phasors. Try the sorter below.

Ohm's law for AC circuits

The rules and equations of DC circuits apply to AC circuits only when the circuit contains resistance alone, as in lamps and heating elements. To use effective values of voltage and current in AC circuits, the effect of inductance and capacitance with resistance must be considered. The combined effects of resistance, inductive reactance and capacitive reactance make up the total opposition to current flow, called impedance, symbol Z, unit the ohm.

If an AC circuit has resistance only, Z equals R and Ohm's law I = E/Z is exactly the same as for DC.

I = EZ    (Z = R when there is resistance only)

Resistance and reactance cannot be added directly. They act at right angles, so they combine like the sides of a right triangle.

Power in AC circuits

Volts times amps is not always watts. Find out where the missing power goes.

Voltage and current determine power, so AC and DC power are similar, but in AC the current depends on both resistance and reactance. The power in any AC circuit depends on the applied voltage and on the circuit's resistance and reactance. An AC circuit has two distinct types of power: one produced by the resistance and one produced by the reactance.

a · Phase angle and power factor

The resistive power and the reactive power act at right angles (Fig 3-21). Power factor (PF) is the ratio of true power to apparent power. It is tied to the phase shift: the greater the phase shift, the lower the power factor. A purely inductive circuit has a phase shift of 90° and a power factor of 0. A purely resistive circuit has no phase shift and a power factor of 1 (100%).

PF = True power (W)Apparent power (VA) × 100 %    = cos φ

b · Apparent, reactive and true power

  • Apparent power (S): what the whole circuit appears to consume, resistance and reactance together. Unit: volt-amp (VA). S = E × I, or S = I² × Z.
  • True power (P): the working power of the circuit, consumed by the resistance. Unit: watt (W). Indicated by any wattmeter. P = I² × R.
  • Reactive power (Q): the power swapped back and forth with the reactance. Unit: var. Q = E × I × sin φ. It does no work.
S² = P² + Q²    P = S cos φ    Q = S sin φ
Notes check · p.29The notes call apparent power "sometimes the reactive power". They are different: apparent power is in VA and reactive power is in var. If true power is 8 and apparent power is 10, reactive power is √(10² − 8²) = 6.

A highly capacitive circuit has a large leading phase angle, so its reactive power is greater than its active power. A pure inductor has zero true power, because P = VI cos 90° = 0.

3.3.4 · Notes pp.31–40

Series and parallel resonance

One special frequency makes an inductor and a capacitor cancel each other. Everything about tuned circuits follows from it.

a · Definition

A special case occurs when XC = XL. The two equal and opposite reactances cancel and the circuit behaves as if only resistance R were present, so Z = R. The circuit is then resonant. XL rises with frequency and XC falls with it, so there is only one frequency where they are equal. If R is small, the current at resonance can become very high.

Set XL = XC and solve for f:

2πfL = 12πfC  →  f² = 14π²LC  →  fr = 12π√LC   or   ωr = 1√LC

fr is in hertz when L is in henries and C in farads.

Series resonance (acceptor circuit)

  • The current is greatest, because Z = R only.
  • V = VR, VL = VC, Z = R, XL = XC.
  • VL and VC are equal and can be many times the supply voltage.
  • Voltage and current are in phase, so the power factor is unity.
  • Called an acceptor circuit because its low impedance accepts current easily.

Impedance vectors (Fig 3-24): if XL > XC the net reactance XL − XC points up and Z is larger than R (inductive). If XL < XC it points down (capacitive). If XL = XC, Z = R.

Parallel resonance (rejector circuit)

If XL and XC are equal in an R, L, C parallel circuit, the external current equals IR. If only L and C were present, the external current would be zero, but a large circulating current flows round the L-C loop. IL and IC are equal and opposite, so the supply gives up no current. A parallel L-C circuit has infinite impedance at resonance and is called a rejector. The same formula fr applies to series and parallel.

b · Impedance, current and phase angle across frequency

Below resonance the series circuit is capacitive (current leads), above it is inductive (current lags), and at resonance the phase angle is zero. The impedance curve (Fig 3-29) has its sharp feature at fr: for the series circuit Z falls to R, for the parallel circuit Z rises to its peak.

c · Frequency response and d · bandwidth, Q

The quality factor Q measures the "goodness" of a resonant circuit. A higher Q gives a narrower bandwidth, which is desirable in many uses. Q is the power stored divided by the power dissipated:

Q = PstoredPdissipated = I²XI²R = XR    BW = frQ

X is the reactance at resonance and R the series resistance. In a series resonant circuit the voltage across L or C is Q times the applied voltage. In a parallel resonant circuit the current through L or C is Q times the total current. Bandwidth is measured between the 0.707 current points, which are the half-power points because (0.707)² = 0.5. Figure 3-31 shows that a high-Q circuit has a narrow bandwidth.

e · Tank circuit (Fig 3-32)

A parallel L-C circuit is a tank circuit. Example: 10 µF with 100 mH. fr = 1 ÷ (2π√(0.1 × 0.00001)) = 1 ÷ (2π × 0.001) = 159 Hz. The most common use is tuning radio transmitters and receivers: the LC circuit is set to resonate at the carrier frequency of the station. A parallel resonant circuit can also be the load impedance in RF amplifier outputs.

3.4 · Notes pp.40–46

Filters: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-stop

A filter uses the fact that reactance changes with frequency to let some frequencies through and stop the rest.

Filter circuits are four-terminal networks designed to pass a band of frequencies from input to output and to attenuate the unwanted ones. They are made from capacitors and inductors, whose reactance changes with frequency. Low-pass and high-pass filters work through XL = 2πfL (proportional to f) and XC = 1/2πfC (inversely proportional to f).

a · Low-pass filter (Figs 3-33, 3-34)

Passes all frequencies below the cut-off frequency (foH) and blocks those above. The inductor in the series arm has low reactance for low frequencies and high reactance for high ones. The capacitor across the line has low reactance for high frequencies, so they leak away through it, but high reactance for low frequencies, so those are not weakened. Several sections in succession sharpen the cut-off.

b · High-pass filter (Figs 3-35, 3-36)

Passes all frequencies above the cut-on frequency (foL) and blocks those below. The capacitor in the series arm passes high frequencies and offers high reactance to low ones. The inductor across the line has low reactance for low frequencies, which are shunted away, and high reactance for high ones. Cascaded stages make the cut-on more abrupt.

c · Band-pass (Fig 3-37)

Passive and active filters (pp.45–46)

Passive

R, C and L only

No amplifying element, so no power supply. Simplest in component count. Not limited by op-amp bandwidth, so good at very high frequency and at larger currents and voltages. Low noise (only thermal noise of the resistors).

Drawbacks: no gain, input and output impedances may be poor so buffers can be needed, inductors are large and costly at high accuracy, and passive filters above 2nd order are difficult and time-consuming to design.

Active

Op amps with R and C

Use amplifying elements, especially op amps, with resistors and capacitors in the feedback loops. Can have high input impedance, low output impedance and any gain, and are usually easier to design. They lack inductors, which removes those problems.

Drawbacks: limited by the amplifier's gain-bandwidth product, and they add amplifier noise. Fig 3-39 shows a second-order Sallen-Key low-pass: two resistors and two capacitors on the + input set cut-off and Q, two resistors on the − input set gain.

Filters from scratch: one resistor and one capacitor (or coil)

Start with the simplest filter that exists and ask why at every step. Everything later in this chapter (tanks, ladders, op-amps) is this idea made sharper.

1 · Why filter at all?

A signal never travels alone. Along with the wanted wave come unwanted ones: 50 Hz hum from the mains, hiss from resistors and amplifiers, a neighbouring radio station, and the high harmonics that make a square wave sharp but also make it pick up interference. A filter is a circuit whose gain depends on frequency, so it can keep the wanted frequencies and shrink the rest. The whole trick rests on one fact you already know.

2 · The one idea: reactance changes with frequency

XC = 12πfC    (falls as f rises)   ·   XL = 2πfL    (rises as f rises)
f very low (towards DC)f very high
CapacitorXC huge: acts like a gap (open circuit)XC tiny: acts like a wire (short circuit)
InductorXL tiny: acts like a wireXL huge: acts like a gap

Why? A capacitor passes current only while its voltage is changing (I = C × dV/dt). A slow signal changes slowly, so little current flows. A fast one changes quickly, so much current flows. An inductor is the mirror image: it resists changes of current, so fast signals are held back.

3 · Low-pass: a voltage divider with a part that changes

Put R in series with C and take the output across C. This is the voltage divider from the Foundations chapter, except the lower part is a reactance. R and XC are 90° apart (the resistor voltage is in step with the current, the capacitor voltage lags it), so their voltages are two sides of a right triangle, and the input is the hypotenuse:

VoutVin = XC√R² + XC² = 1√1 + (f ÷ fc)²   with   fc = 12πRC

Low f: XC ≫ R, the capacitor takes almost all the voltage, so the signal passes. High f: XC ≪ R, the capacitor shorts the signal and almost nothing is left. Slide the test tone in the lab and watch the triangle: the purple side (the output) shrinks as the frequency rises.

4 · Where is the cut-off, and why 0.707?

The “edge” of the filter is defined where the two oppositions are equal, R = XC. Then Z = √(R² + R²) = √2 R, so each voltage is 1/√2 = 0.707 of the input and the phase is exactly 45°. Power is V²/R, so half the power is left: that is the −3 dB point. Solving R = 1/(2πfcC) gives fc = 1/(2πRC), the same number that ends up in every formula above. Nothing special happens to the circuit at fc; people simply agreed to call “half power” the edge.

5 · High-pass: swap which part you listen to

Take the same two parts but swap the order: C in series, output across R. Now the output is the other side of the same triangle. At high f the capacitor is a wire, so everything reaches R. At low f the capacitor is a gap and nothing does. The gain is (f/fc) ÷ √(1 + (f/fc)²), the mirror image of the low-pass. A capacitor in series is also exactly why AC coupling blocks DC.

6 · The coil versions (RL)

An inductor behaves oppositely, so an RL low-pass has L in series and the output across R, and an RL high-pass has R in series and the output across L. The shape is identical; only the cut-off formula changes, fc = R ÷ (2πL). Coils are bigger, heavier, pick up magnetic interference and have wire resistance, so at low power RC is preferred. RL appears where a coil is already there: a motor winding, a power choke, a filter in a switching supply.

7 · Reading the Bode picture: decibels and decades

Gain ranges from 1 down to 0.001, so we plot it in decibels, dB = 20 log10(gain), against a log frequency axis. Gain 0.707 is −3 dB, 0.1 is −20 dB, 0.01 is −40 dB. Far above fc the low-pass gain is about fc/f, so every ×10 in frequency divides the gain by 10, which is −20 dB per decade (about −6 dB per octave). A single RC stage has this gentle slope, which is why one stage is a poor “wall”. The phase swings smoothly from 0° to −90° (low-pass) because the capacitor voltage lags the current by up to 90°.

8 · Preview: a square wave is a pile of sines

Switch on “square wave” in the lab. A low-pass rounds the sharp corners because the corners are made of high-frequency sine components, and these are the ones shrunk. A high-pass lets only the edges through and the flat tops sag. That is the first hint of the last chapter, where the harmonics are pulled out of a signal one by one.

Exam linkQuestions asking “which circuit passes high frequencies?” are answered by asking which part is in series: capacitor in series (output across R) is high-pass, resistor in series (output across C) is low-pass. For inductors it is the opposite.

Band-pass and band-stop with L and C, and why tank circuits replace RC

Two reactances that cancel give you a filter with a sharp, adjustable centre. This chapter shows both ways to build it, and then shows honestly why a real tank is never as good as the drawing.

1 · Why two parts make a band

XL rises and XC falls. They are equal at exactly one frequency, f0 = 1 ÷ (2π√(LC)). Because they act in opposite directions, the net reactance XL − XC passes through zero there. Put the pair in series and the impedance dips to nothing at f0. Put them in parallel and the impedance climbs towards infinity at f0. The lab’s lower-left graph shows exactly this on a log scale: look where the purple curve dives or peaks, right at the crossing of the two dashed lines.

ArrangementAt f₀ the LC part is…ResultOld name
Series LC in the linea wire (Z ≈ 0)Band-passacceptor
Parallel LC to grounda gap (Z huge)Band-passtank, tuned shunt
Parallel LC in the linea gap (Z huge)Band-stoprejector, wave trap

So there are two ways of making each type, one using the series property and one using the parallel property. The rule is: in the line + low Z = passes, to ground + low Z = dumps.

2 · How sharp? Q and bandwidth

The band is narrow because away from f0 one reactance grows quickly while the other shrinks. Sharpness is Q = X ÷ R, and bandwidth BW = f0 ÷ Q. In the lab, raise Q and the peak narrows while the part values stay tied to f0 and Q: the coil needs to be Q times bigger relative to the resistance it drives. The lab measures the −3 dB points from the real curve, so you can compare with the formula.

3 · Why a tank replaces two RC filters

You can make a band-pass from a high-pass and a low-pass in series. It works, but it can never be sharp. A network of only resistors and capacitors has a Q of at most 0.5. The reason is physical: a resistor only throws energy away. A capacitor stores energy and gives it back, but nothing in an RC network can pass that energy to a second store and back again, so it cannot “ring”. A capacitor and an inductor can: energy sloshes from the electric field of C to the magnetic field of L and back, losing only what the small coil resistance burns. That is a high Q and it is why tuned circuits can pick one radio station out of hundreds.

4 · Why a real tank is inaccurate

The ideal parallel LC has infinite impedance at f0, zero loss and a perfect centre. Real tanks fall short in six ways. Turn each one on in the lab and see the effect:

  • The coil has wire resistance. This is the main loss. It limits the coil’s own Q (about 50 to 200 for ordinary coils) and lowers the peak.
  • The source and the load leak energy. A load connected to the tank is in parallel with it and acts like extra resistance. The budget bar shows 1/Qtotal = 1/Qcoil + 1/Qsource + 1/Qload: the weakest link sets the Q.
  • Tolerance. f0 ∝ 1/√(LC), so ±5 % parts give about ±5 % in frequency. Tick “14 builds” to see the spread of grey curves. This is why production tuned circuits have an adjustable core or trimmer.
  • Drift. Ferrite cores and capacitors change with temperature and age, so f0 wanders.
  • Slow skirts. One resonator falls only about 6 dB per octave away from the peak, so unwanted stations nearby are only weakly rejected. Sharper rejection needs several tuned stages (the next chapter).
  • Stray parts. Every wire has a little L and C; at high frequencies they shift f0 and add unwanted resonances.
Rule of thumbA tank is great at being selective around one frequency. It is poor at being accurate unless the parts are good and the loading is light. That is why crystals (Q of tens of thousands) and active filters took over where accuracy matters.

Cascading T and π networks: towards a perfect filter, and what it costs

One section is gentle. Chain sections and the skirt gets steeper. Keep going and you approach the dream brick wall, but the bill arrives in coils.

1 · Two ways to draw one section: T and π

A basic low-pass section has one series coil and one shunt capacitor. To keep the section symmetrical (the same from either end) we split the odd part in half. Split the coil and you get a T (L/2, C, L/2). Split the capacitor and you get a π (C/2, L, C/2), named after the Greek letter. They are two drawings of the same filter family and have identical behaviour in the middle; they only differ in which part touches the outside world.

2 · Design equations (why these formulas)

For a section meant to work between two resistances R0 the two requirements are the cut-off fc and the “characteristic impedance” √(L ÷ C) = R0 (so the section looks like a plain resistor R0 to its neighbours). Together they give

L = R0π fc   ·   C = 1π R0 fc   ·   fc = 1π√LC

Notice L ∝ 1/fc: low cut-off frequencies need big coils. Remember it for the bulk problem below.

3 · Cascading: why sections add steepness

Each LC section has two reactive parts, so each extra section adds about 12 dB per octave to the skirt. Two sections side by side put two half-coils next to each other, which simply merge into one full coil, so N sections need only N + 1 coils. In the lab, slide the number of sections up and watch the purple curve march towards the green dream line.

4 · Why simple k-constant sections are not perfect

The characteristic impedance √(L/C) only equals R0 well in the middle of the passband. Near fc it drifts, so the filter and the load no longer match. The result is ripple in the passband and a rounded “knee” at the cut-off, and the more sections you add the more visible the ripple becomes. Meanwhile coil resistance adds loss in the passband too (drag Qcoil down).

5 · Better recipes: Butterworth and Chebyshev

Instead of repeating one section, choose each part value from a table (the “g-values”) so the whole ladder follows a designed curve. Butterworth gives the flattest possible passband; Chebyshev allows controlled ripple (1 dB here) in return for a steeper skirt for the same number of parts. Compare them in the lab at order 5: the Chebyshev has the sharper knee, the Butterworth the cleaner passband. This is the first optimisation: same size, better curve.

6 · Can a filter be perfect?

A perfect filter would have a vertical wall at fc: 0 dB below and −∞ above. Mathematics says that would need infinitely many parts and would delay the signal by an infinite time (it would have to know the future). Every real filter is a compromise among four things: steepness, passband flatness, phase (timing) accuracy and size. Improve one and another pays.

7 · The bulk problem, and the ways out

Watch the “price” panel in the lab: each added section adds another coil, and with a realistic coil Q the passband loss climbs too. At audio frequencies the coils are henries and kilograms. The common optimisations:

Why we filter: harmonics, noise, and the op-amp

Pull a signal apart into its sine waves and you can see exactly what noise is, why it sticks to the signal, and how a filter removes it.

1 · Every signal is a pile of sine waves

Any repeating wave is a sum of sines: the fundamental (the repeat frequency) plus harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4× … A square wave is 1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + 1/7 … (odd harmonics only). The sharp corners of the square are made by the high harmonics. The spectrum panel in the lab draws each sine as one bar. Tap a blue bar to pull that harmonic out of the signal: with the 3rd and 5th gone, the flat top starts to wobble and the corners round off. Pull them all and only the sine fundamental is left.

2 · How noise arrives with the signal

Noise is nothing mysterious: it is more sine waves at the wrong frequencies.

  • Mains hum (50 Hz and its harmonics): the wires act as little antennas for the mains field. It appears as red bars at 50, 100 and 150 Hz.
  • Interference: radio transmitters, switching power supplies, motors. A single tall red bar at some high frequency (14 kHz in the lab).
  • Thermal (Johnson) noise: inside every resistor, electrons jiggle because it is warm, producing a random voltage v = √(4kTRΔf), with k = 1.38 × 10−23 J/K and T in kelvin. It has equal power at all frequencies, which is why it appears as the grey “forest” of bars: many small random sines at every frequency (we call this white noise, like white light containing every colour).
  • Amplifier noise: every transistor and op-amp adds its own hiss, quoted as a noise density in nV/√Hz.
  • Distortion harmonics: a non-linear stage turns a clean sine into a sine plus unwanted harmonics.

These add on top of the wanted bars. The ear or the scope sees the sum, which is why the top-left trace looks fuzzy even though the dashed wanted wave is clean.

3 · How a filter improves the signal-to-noise ratio

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) = 10 log (signal power ÷ noise power). The filter multiplies each bar by its own gain, so a band-pass around the wanted bars throws away the red bars outside it and most of the grey ones. Because white noise has power in every hertz, noise power is proportional to bandwidth: halve the bandwidth and you get half the noise power (−3 dB) for free. The price is that the filter must not cut the wanted harmonics, or the signal changes shape. Try it: a low-pass above the 15th harmonic keeps the square wave sharp but lets the 14 kHz whine through; a tighter low-pass kills the whine but rounds the corners. That is the signal-versus-noise trade-off every filter designer makes.

4 · The op-amp: filters without coils

An operational amplifier is a high-gain amplifier that, with feedback, behaves like an ideal voltage copier or amplifier. In an active filter the filtering is done by resistors and capacitors, and the op-amp supplies three things that passive filters lack:

  • Buffering. The input is high-impedance and the output is low-impedance, so a following stage cannot load the filter (remember how loading wrecked the tank).
  • Sharpness without coils. Feedback through a capacitor creates a high Q using only R and C. This beats the “RC can never exceed Q = 0.5” limit because the op-amp supplies energy.
  • Gain. The filter can amplify as well as filter.

The lab shows the common Sallen-Key two-pole filter. With equal resistors, fc = 1 ÷ (2πR√(C1C2)) and Q = ½√(C1/C2), so the capacitor ratio sets the shape: Q = 0.707 is the flat Butterworth, Q = 4 shows a peak.

Limits of active filtersThey need a power supply (the exam’s “passive filters do not need power” is the other side of this coin). The op-amp is only fast up to its gain-bandwidth; the grey versus purple curves in the lab show it failing at the top. Big resistors add hiss and the op-amp adds its own noise, so the density panel shows what survives. Narrowing the filter reduces the total noise, but you cannot filter away noise that is in the same band as the signal.

Three-phase fundamentals

Three coils, 120° apart. See the generator, then see why star and delta connections bring √3 into the maths.

Why more than one phase

The simplest way to distribute AC uses two wires (the third wire in a home is only an earth). In many practical uses, including aircraft, a multiphase supply is better than single-phase. Here "phase" means an AC voltage source that may not rise and fall at the same time as other sources present.

The most common system has three separate voltage sources and three wires: three phase. The voltages are spaced equally in time, 120° (360°/3) apart. All three are sine waves of the same frequency and periodic time (Fig 3-40).

The generator (Figs 3-41, 3-42)

A single loop turned in a magnetic field gives single-phase AC. Add two more loops spaced 120° apart, each with its own slip rings and brushes, and you have a three-output generator. The phases are named a, b, c and colour-coded red, yellow, blue. At the start of the graph, loop a is parallel to the field so it generates no voltage, loop b is approaching its maximum negative voltage and loop c is falling from positive towards zero. Practical generators replace the loop by a winding and connect the windings in ways that also cut the number of slip rings.

3.5.1 · Line and phase quantities

Phase voltage is the voltage across one winding. Line voltage is the voltage between two of the three lines. Phase current flows in a winding, line current in a line.

3.5.2 · Star (wye) and delta connections

Delta (Δ)

Three wires

Phase ends are paired start to finish, so only three terminals are needed. The phase-winding voltages are the line voltages.

VL = VP    IL = √3 · IP

Line currents lag their phase voltages for inductive loads. Notes example: phase current 100 A gives line current 174 A.

Star (Y)

Three wires and a neutral

One end of each winding joins at the star point (neutral). With equal loads the vector sum Ia + Ib + Ic is zero, so the neutral current is zero: a balanced load.

VL = √3 · VP    IL = IP

The line voltage is 30° ahead of the leading phase voltage of the pair it comes from.

Where √3 comes from (pp.49–53)

By Kirchhoff's second law, Eab = Ean − Ebn = Ean + (−Ebn). Add two vectors of equal size VP that are 60° apart (Ean and −Ebn). The parallelogram diagonal splits into two equal halves at 30°:

VL = 2 · VP · cos 30° = 2 · VP · √32 = √3 · VP

For the delta currents, Kirchhoff's first law gives Ib = Ibc − Iab. The same geometry, two equal vectors 120° apart and a bisector, gives IL = 2 · IP · cos 30° = √3 · IP. The line current is also 30° behind the lagging phase current of its pair.

3.5.3 · Power in three-phase systems

For an unbalanced system, total power is the sum of the phase powers: P = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ = V₁I₁cosφ₁ + V₂I₂cosφ₂ + V₃I₃cosφ₃. For a balanced load it is simply 3 · VP · IP · cos φ. Using the √3 relations, for both star and delta:

P = √3 · VL · IL · cos φ    (power factor = cos φ)
Example 1: VL = 110 V, IL = 12 A, PF = 0.8 P = √3 × 110 × 12 × 0.8 = 1829 W = 1.829 kW Example 2: delta, line current 6 A IP = IL ÷ √3 = 6 ÷ 1.732 = 3.46 A

Transformers

Two coils that never touch, yet power crosses between them. Turn the 3D transformer and change the number of turns.

Principle (Fig 3.53)

A transformer transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another through inductively coupled conductors. A changing current in the primary winding creates a changing magnetic field. That field induces a changing voltage in the secondary winding. With a load across the secondary, current flows. The output voltage is set by the input voltage and the ratio of turns. High voltage converted to low voltage is step-down, the reverse is step-up.

Both windings are wound on a common low-reluctance core of steel laminations, so nearly all the alternating flux produced by the primary links the secondary. A sinusoidal primary current produces sinusoidal flux in the core:

Φ = Φmax sin(ωt)

The induced emf depends on the rate of change of flux, so it follows the flux like a cosine: it lags the flux by 90°. The working principle is mutual inductance.

3.6.1 · Construction (Fig 3.54)

  • Core: laminated steel, a low-reluctance path for the main flux. A small leakage flux does not link both coils.
  • Core type (Fig 3.55): U-shaped and I- or L-shaped laminations, staggered when assembled to make one circular magnetic circuit. Windings may be on one limb or split between two.
  • Shell type (Fig 3.56): E/T and U-shaped laminations staggered to give a three-limbed core. For single phase, both windings go on the centre limb. For three phase, each phase has its own limb. More expensive, but the two magnetic paths make it better for large currents.

3.6.2 · Symbols, turns ratio and polarity markings

Dot codes (Fig 3.57). The two dots show the phase relationship between the windings: terminals with a dot are always in phase with each other. Dots on the same side give no phase change. Dots on opposite corners give a 180° phase change between primary and secondary voltage.

Core markings. Three lines between the windings mean a laminated iron core, used at low frequency such as AC power supply systems. Dashed lines mean a ferrite core, used at medium to high frequencies. No line means an air core, used at VHF and above. Fig 3.58 shows two secondaries on one laminated core, with dots showing they are wound in opposite directions: the top of one is positive while the top of the other is negative.

Fig 3.60 colour code. A power transformer's primary has black leads. Secondaries: red leads for the high-voltage winding (red-yellow is its centre tap), yellow for the 5 V winding (yellow-blue centre tap) and green for the 6 V winding (green-yellow centre tap).

3.6.3 · Primary and secondary voltages and currents

E₂E₁ = N₂N₁   →   E₂ = E₁ · N₂N₁

E₁ is the primary voltage, E₂ the secondary voltage, N₁ and N₂ the turns. Transformers do not create power, so volts × amps stays the same: current changes the opposite way to voltage. In terms of ampere-turns:

IP NP = IS NS   →   IprimaryIsecondary = NsecondaryNprimary = t (turns ratio)

3.6.4 · Losses and efficiency

Transformer losses are very small, 98% efficiency being easily reached, but some loss occurs in all of them. Efficiency is output power divided by input power. The losses fall into three groups:

  • Iron (core) losses, in two kinds.
    • Hysteresis: continually magnetising and demagnetising the core costs energy that is dissipated as heat. It depends on frequency and core material, rising with frequency or flux density. Cores use materials with a narrow hysteresis loop: stalloy, permalloy, mumetal.
    • Eddy current: emfs induced in the metal core drive circulating currents that heat it. Small currents give little loss, but if they can join up the large circulating currents waste a lot, and the loss is proportional to the square of the frequency. Laminating the core keeps them small.
  • Copper losses: the I²R losses in the windings. They depend on the currents and winding resistance, not on frequency. They also drop the secondary voltage when loaded.
  • Flux leakage losses: flux that escapes and does not link both windings.

3.6.5 · No-load and load conditions

No load. The primary has losses from resistance, hysteresis and eddy currents. They create a loss current in phase with the applied voltage. The core and coils make the primary highly inductive, so the magnetising current lags the applied voltage by 90°. The primary current with the secondary off-load is the vector sum of the two. The magnetising current produces the field, which lags the voltage by 90°. The induced secondary emf lags the field by another 90°, so the secondary voltage is 180° out of phase with the applied voltage (Fig 3.61).

Because the primary reactance is large, no-load primary current is small. At a lower frequency the reactance falls and the primary current rises, so transformers must not be run below rated frequency without reducing the applied voltage.

Load. A load current in the secondary sets up a flux that opposes the primary flux and lowers the primary inductance. With constant applied voltage, that raises the primary current: the load component. It sets up a flux equal and opposite to the secondary flux, so the ampere-turns balance. The total primary current is the vector sum of no-load and load currents. Normally the load current is so much larger that the no-load current can be ignored.

3.6.6 · Applications

a

Power transformer

It does not add electricity, it only changes voltage and current. Ideally no power is lost. A voltage increase means a current decrease. Power is stepped up at the source to cut line loss over long distances, and down at the point of use because motors, lights and appliances cannot use high voltage.

b

Instrument transformers

Used to measure voltage, current, power, energy, power factor and frequency in AC systems, and with protective relays. They step down system voltage and current. Meters are generally designed for 5 A and 110 V.

c

Voltage (potential) transformer

Steps system voltage down to a safe value for meters and relays. The primary connects across phase and ground. It has fewer turns on its secondary, like any step-down transformer.

d

Current transformer

A ring-type transformer whose primary is the power lead or ground lead of the generator. It supplies a current proportional to line current for protection and control. Sides are marked H1 and H2; H1 faces the generator. Never leave the secondary open while the system runs: dangerously high voltage and overheating result. Fit a jumper when it is unused.

e · Autotransformer (Fig 3.62)

An electrical transformer with only one winding. "Auto" is Greek for "self". The winding has at least three taps. Because part of the winding does double duty it is smaller, lighter and cheaper than a two-winding transformer, with lower leakage reactance, lower losses, lower excitation current and more VA for its size. The disadvantage: no electrical isolation between primary and secondary. It is often used to step between the 110–120 V and 220–240 V ranges, for example giving 110 V or 120 V from a 230 V supply. The supply and autotransformer must be rated for the power required.

Why the technician must understand the meter

The best and most expensive meter is no use unless the technician knows what is being measured and what each reading means. A meter must not change the circuit it is connected to. Meters are either self-excited (powered from a source inside the meter) or externally excited (powered by the circuit they are connected to). The common analog meters are the voltmeter, ammeter and ohmmeter, and all work on electromagnetism: the interaction between the magnetic field of a permanent magnet and the field of a current-carrying coil. A stronger coil current gives a stronger field and more rotation.

4.1.1 · The D'Arsonval meter movement

This basic DC movement, first used by the French scientist d'Arsonval, is a current-measuring device used in the ammeter, voltmeter and ohmmeter. The pointer is deflected in proportion to the current through the coil. The ammeter and voltmeter are both current-measuring instruments; the difference is how they are connected in the circuit. The ohmmeter also measures current, but it provides its own power (self-excited) and contains auxiliary circuits.

Current sensitivity and resistance

The current sensitivity is the current needed for full-scale deflection. A 1 mA movement needs 1 mA for a full-scale reading and 0.5 mA for half scale. The movement resistance is the actual DC resistance of the wire used for the coil.

What the spiral springs doThe coil sits in the magnet's field on a pivot. Two fine spiral hairsprings give the controlling torque: the further the coil turns, the harder the springs push back, so the pointer settles where the magnetic torque and spring torque balance. The springs also carry the current into and out of the coil. Damping keeps the pointer from swinging back and forth.

4.1.2 · Notes pp.5–12

Construction and use of DC meters

One sensitive movement becomes an ammeter with a shunt, a voltmeter with a multiplier, or an ohmmeter with a battery.

i · The ammeter and its shunt (Figs 4.1, 4.2)

A d'Arsonval movement may have 1 mA sensitivity and 50 Ω resistance. To measure more than 1 mA it needs a shunt resistor in parallel, which bypasses the current above the movement's limit. For a 10 mA range the shunt carries 9 mA and the movement 1 mA. They are in parallel, so the voltage across both is the same: ISH · RSH = IM · RM.

RSH = IM × RMISH   →   10 mA range: 1 mA × 50 Ω9 mA = 5.56 Ω   ·   100 mA range: 1 mA × 50 Ω99 mA ≈ 0.51 Ω

A multirange ammeter switches in a different shunt for each range (Fig 4.2 shows 10 mA and 100 mA).

Ammeter precautions
  1. Connect the ammeter in series with the element whose current is measured.
  2. Never connect it across a source of voltage such as a battery or generator. Its resistance is extremely low, especially on high ranges, so a volt or so can cause very high current and damage it.
  3. Use a range large enough to keep the deflection less than full scale. Estimate the current first, or start at the highest range and work down. The best accuracy is near half scale. Many milliammeters have been ruined trying to measure amperes.
  4. Observe polarity. Current must flow through the coil in the right direction. Reversal deflects the needle backwards and often bends it.

ii · The voltmeter and its multiplier (Figs 4.3, 4.4)

The voltmeter uses the same movement but a different external circuit. A 50 µA, 1 kΩ movement drops 50 µA × 1000 Ω = 50 mV at full scale. To measure higher voltage a multiplier resistor RM is added in series to drop the excess. For a 1 V range:

RM = 1 V − 50 mV50 µA = 950 mV50 µA = 19 kΩ    total = 19 kΩ + 1 kΩ = 20 kΩ

Sensitivity is resistance per volt (Ω/V): this meter has 20 kΩ/V. For a 50 µA movement the sensitivity is 20 kΩ/V on every range, so the total resistance for a 10 V range is 200 kΩ and RM = 200 kΩ − 20 kΩ = 180 kΩ (Fig 4.4).

Connections. A voltmeter goes in parallel with the circuit. If unsure of the voltage, start on the highest range and step down. Observe polarity. Loading: the meter shunts some current away, which changes the circuit and the reading, so use a high-resistance voltmeter.

iii · The ohmmeter (Figs 4.5 to 4.8)

The same movement, plus an internal battery and a variable resistor in series. The leads are connected across the unknown resistance, the battery drives a current through the coil, and the pointer deflects in proportion. With the leads open no current flows: the pointer rests at the left, ∞. With the leads shorted the pointer swings to full scale on the right: 0 Ω. The scale is a back-off scale: values decrease from left to right.

Zero adjustment. The variable resistor sets the current so the pointer reads exactly zero ohms with the leads shorted. It compensates for the battery voltage falling with age. Zero the meter before each use.

Example: 50 µA, 1 kΩ movement, 1.5 V internal battery Total ohmmeter resistance = 1.5 V ÷ 50 µA = 30 kΩ Zero-adjust resistor = 30 kΩ − 1 kΩ = 29 kΩ Connect 120 kΩ: total = 150 kΩ, I = 1.5 ÷ 150 kΩ = 10 µA = 20 % of full scale

Marks on the scale: 30 kΩ at 50% of full scale, 45 kΩ at 40%, 120 kΩ at 20%. Practical ohmmeters have ranges R×1, R×10, R×100, R×1k, R×100k, R×1M. The scale reading is multiplied by the range: a pointer at 20 on R×100 means 2 kΩ. Small resistances need more ohmmeter current, so shunt resistors are switched in for each range, and the shunt equals the centre-scale reading of that range. Some meters use a higher battery voltage for the top range.

4.1.3 · Notes pp.13–22

Construction and use of AC meters

A DC meter on AC reads zero. Four fixes: rectify, use an air-core field, use iron vanes, or use heat.

Why a DC meter reads zero on AC

A DC meter such as an ammeter in an AC circuit indicates zero. The field of the permanent magnet always has the same direction, so the coil follows the polarity of the current: it tries to move one way in the first half cycle and the other way in the second half. The current reverses too fast for the coil to follow, so the coil settles at an average position. The current is equal and opposite in each half, so the average, and the reading, is zero.

a · Rectifier-type AC meter (Figs 4.10 to 4.13)

For AC, a rectifier that converts AC to DC is added. A half-wave rectifier passes only the positive half cycles (pulsating DC on every positive half cycle). A full-wave rectifier passes both halves (pulsating DC on both). The movement responds to the average of the pulsating DC, and the scale is then calibrated to read RMS or peak, as the designer wants (in most cases RMS).

  • Half-wave: a diode in series with the permanent-magnet moving-coil (PMMC) movement and a multiplier resistor. Average current = 0.45 × the RMS value, so AC sensitivity is 0.45 times DC sensitivity.
  • Full-wave (bridge of four diodes): AC sensitivity is 0.9 times DC sensitivity.

Rectifier meters are as accurate as electrodynamometer types and cost far less, so they are preferred. Thermocouple instruments are more delicate but are more widely used at very high frequencies.

b · Electrodynamometer type (Fig 4.14)

Measures AC or DC voltage and current. Same principle as the PMMC meter, but the permanent magnet is replaced by an air-core electromagnet whose field is produced by the same current that flows in the moving coil. With no iron it works for both AC and DC. With the fixed and moving coils in series, whenever the moving-coil current reverses the fixed-coil field reverses too, so the needle always moves clockwise.

c · Moving-iron vane type (Fig 4.15)

Measures AC or DC. It has no permanent magnet. It depends on induced magnetism and uses the repulsion between two concentric iron vanes, one fixed and one movable, inside a solenoid. A pointer is attached to the movable vane. Current magnetises both vanes with the same polarity at the same ends, and like poles repel. The movable vane is rectangular and the fixed vane is tapered, which gives a fairly uniform scale. With no current the movable vane sits opposite the wide part of the fixed vane and the scale reads zero. It is pushed toward the narrow end until the repulsion is balanced by the spring. The push is always in the same direction, so it works on either AC or DC.

  • Damping: an aluminium vane on the shaft moving in a restricted air space.
  • As an ammeter the coil has few turns of heavy wire. As a voltmeter it has many turns of fine wire. Portable voltmeters have built-in series resistance up to 750 V; higher ranges use external multipliers.
  • On DC it has an error from residual magnetism in the vanes; reverse the connections and average the readings. On AC its accuracy is 0.5%. It is used extensively for AC power circuits because it is simple and cheap and no current goes to the moving element.
  • It needs more power than a d'Arsonval meter for full scale, so it is seldom used in high-resistance, low-power circuits.

d · Thermocouple type (Figs 4.16, 4.17)

Two different metals in contact form a thermocouple. The current flows through a heater element, which warms the junction. The junction produces a small DC emf roughly proportional to the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions. A PMMC meter reads it. Because it responds to heating it indicates the RMS value, works on AC and DC, and stays accurate at very high frequency. The bridge type (rated about 100 mA to 1 A AC) uses two thermocouples in a bridge with no separate heater: the current itself heats the junctions (I²R) and the meter shows the potential difference between junctions a and b.

4.1.4 to 4.1.8 · Notes pp.12, 22–26

Multimeters, wattmeters, Wheatstone bridge, meggers and bond testers

Five more instruments, each answering a different question.

4.1.4 · Digital multimeters

Technicians traditionally used the analog voltmeter, ammeter and ohmmeter, combined in one instrument called a multimeter or VOM (volt-ohm-milliammeter). Digital multimeters (DMM) and digital voltmeters (DVM) are now more common. They are easier to read and more accurate, with less ambiguity. The analog multimeter's single-coil movement needs several scales that are not always easy to read, and its internal resistance loads the circuit. A DVM has a higher input resistance, so it loads the circuit less.

4.1.5 · Wattmeters (Fig 4.18)

Electric power is the product of current and voltage, so a wattmeter needs two elements: one for current and one for voltage. It is usually of the electrodynamometer type. The movable coil in series with a resistance is the voltage (potential) element. The stationary coils are the current element. Deflection is proportional to the product of the voltage across the potential coil and the current through the current coils. If the line current and the voltage both reverse, the pointer still reads up-scale, so it measures AC or DC power. On AC it indicates true (active) power: V × I × cos φ.

4.1.6 · Wheatstone bridge (Fig 4.19)

Four resistors in a diamond with a DC input and an output between the two middle nodes. Used in two ways: (1) balance the bridge by adjusting a resistor until the output is zero, to find an unknown resistor when the other three are known; (2) measure the output voltage when one resistor is replaced by a device that reacts to temperature, pressure or shape (strain), to monitor those changes.

4.1.7 · Meggers (insulation testers) (Figs 4.9, 4.20, 4.21)

A megger or megohmmeter is a high-range ohmmeter that measures insulation resistance and other very high resistances. It also checks ground, continuity and short circuits in electrical power systems. Its advantage over an ohmmeter is that it measures resistance at a high potential, so insulation is stressed and will show if it breaks down or leaks. Types: electronic (battery operated), manual (hand cranked) and motor-operated (needs an outside source to turn a motor that turns the generator).

Construction. Two parts share a permanent magnet: (1) a hand-driven DC generator G, and (2) the instrument portion of the opposed-coil (ratio) type. Coils A and B are fixed to the same moving member in a magnetic field. Coil B tends to turn the pointer counter-clockwise, coil A clockwise. Coil A is in series with R3 and the unknown Rx. Coil B is in series with R2 and sits across the generator. There are no restoring springs, so the pointer floats freely when the generator is not running.

  • Terminals open: no current in A, coil B alone turns the pointer to ∞.
  • Resistance connected: current in A grows, the pointer comes to rest where the two torques balance. This depends on the ratio of the currents, so the reading is independent of the voltage.
  • Terminals shorted: current in A is large and the pointer rests at zero. R3 limits the current so the instrument is not damaged.

Crank types. A variable-pressure megger's voltage depends on how fast you turn the crank. A constant-pressure megger uses a centrifugal governor or slip clutch which holds voltage constant above its slip speed.

Insulation resistance test. A spot test with an applied DC voltage, typically 250, 500 or 1000 V for low-voltage equipment (below 600 V), and 2500 or 5000 V for high-voltage equipment. Results are in kΩ, MΩ or GΩ. Used to verify the insulation of motors, cables, generator windings and similar. Parts: analog display, hand crank, wire leads. The electronic type adds a digital display, selector switch and indicator.

4.1.8 · Bonding testers (Fig 4.22)

Adhesive bonding to join metals, composites, plastics and other materials is increasing in many industries. The aircraft industry widely adopted metal-to-metal bonding after World War II, and it has spread to aerospace, automotive, marine and wind energy. A handheld bond tester has three modes of operation. It can detect delaminations, disbonds, crushed or damaged core, porosity and foreign objects in monolithic, sandwich and bonded structures.

4.2 · Notes pp.26–33

General avionics test equipment

Logic probe, oscilloscope, frequency counter and signal generators.

a · Logic probe (Fig 4.23)

A hand-held, pen-like probe used to analyse and troubleshoot the logic states (Boolean 0 or 1) of a digital circuit. It is low cost, portable and easy to use for simple maintenance on low-complexity logic. Most are powered by the circuit under test, some use batteries. An LED shows up to four states:

  • Logic high: usually red.
  • Logic low: usually green.
  • Pulses: a third colour, possibly amber. It may detect very short pulses, and the brightness may show pulse length.
  • Tri-stated line: the output is turned off and no real state is defined. All indicators are off.

b · Oscilloscope (Figs 4.24 to 4.27)

The scope lets you see and quantify waveform features such as phase, amplitude and duration. Its CRT is a vacuum instrument with an electron gun that fires a narrow focused beam at a phosphorescent screen, which emits light where the beam strikes it.

  • The heated cathode emits electrons. The control grid voltage sets how many pass, so it controls the beam intensity.
  • The acceleration anodes speed the electrons up. The focusing anode narrows the beam to a fine point. The screen surface is also an anode.
  • Deflection plates bend the beam to a specific point (Fig 4.25). A zero voltage leaves the beam alone, a negative voltage repels it and a positive voltage attracts it.

Horizontal deflection. An internal sawtooth voltage goes to the horizontal plates. It starts negative and rises at a constant rate to positive, so the beam sweeps from far left to far right. The rate sets the sweep rate. When the sweep reaches the right, the beam returns quickly to the left. During this flyback the beam is blanked and draws nothing. Vertical deflection. The input signal goes to the vertical plates. Both together trace the waveform (Fig 4.27).

Controls. Horizontal position and vertical position move the trace. Focus narrows the beam so it is not fuzzy. Intensity is brightness: do not leave it high for long tests or with a motionless dot, which can damage the screen.

c · Frequency meters and counters (Fig 4.28)

A digital frequency meter displays the frequency of a periodic signal, to three decimal places of accuracy. It counts the oscillations during a preset interval, shows the value, then resets to zero. Block diagram: a sine-to-pulse converter, a gate, a counter, a clock (time base) and a display. The gate is a switch: pulses pass only while it is open. The time-base flip-flop opens and closes the gate. The counter counts pulses that pass while it is open. When the next pulse reaches the flip-flop the interval ends, the count is displayed and a reset pulse clears the counters. For low frequencies, deflection-type meters are common: resonant-circuit types and ratio meters.

d · Audio-frequency (AF) signal generator

An electronic device that creates sound waveforms at different frequencies across a broad spectrum. It produces sub-audio, audio and ultrasonic frequencies and sine, square and triangle waves. Most have an output-level control. It is sometimes used to test hearing.

e · Radio-frequency (RF) signal generator (Figs 4.29, 4.30)

Widely used in RF and microwave design and test. Block diagram: oscillator (the most important block, normally a frequency synthesizer, set by commands from the controller), amplifier (raises the signal to a fixed level, with a level-control loop to hold output constant across frequency and temperature), attenuator (keeps an accurate source impedance and sets the output level very accurately; the output impedance is less accurate at the highest levels, where there is least attenuation) and a processor (takes front-panel and remote commands and controls everything).

4.3 · Notes pp.33–42

Basic circuit troubleshooting using test equipment

Find a failed part by reasoning from what the meter shows. Then try it on a circuit with a hidden fault.

Troubleshooting is the systematic process of recognising the symptoms of a problem, identifying the possible cause and locating the failed component or conductor. You must understand how the circuit works and how to use the equipment.

TermMeaning
Short circuitAn unintentional low-resistance path between two components, or between a component or conductor and ground. Usually creates high current that burns out or damages conductors or components.
Open circuitA circuit that is not a complete, continuous path: infinitely large resistance. Switches open and close circuits on purpose. A failed bulb or burned-out resistor opens a circuit by accident.
ContinuityThe state of being continuous, uninterrupted, connected together.
DiscontinuityThe opposite: the circuit is broken.

4.3.1 · Voltage and current measurement (Fig 4.31)

Voltage is measured across a component, in parallel. Check the meter is set for DC or AC as needed. Red lead in the + jack, black in the − jack, and match the component's polarity. If the size is uncertain, start on the highest range and go down until you read near mid-scale, the most accurate region. All meters shunt some current, mostly a concern for older analog meters.

Current is measured by opening the circuit and inserting the ammeter in series. The + side of the meter faces the positive source. The meter's small internal resistance has a minor effect that can usually be neglected.

4.3.2 · Continuity checks and resistance (Figs 4.32, 4.33)

Zero the ohmmeter first. Open leads: needle to the left (infinite). Shorted leads: needle to the right (zero). To check a resistor, disconnect it from the circuit, otherwise the circuit is in parallel with it and the reading is wrong, and the meter could be damaged. Continuity: a good connection reads near zero ohms. An open, or a poor connection such as an over-crimped pin, reads infinity or very high resistance. Keep your fingers off the probes, because your body is another parallel path.

4.3.3 · Capacitor and inductor checks (Figs 4.34)

Capacitor. Two usual failures: a short (dielectric breakdown) or an open; a slower failure is degradation. Remove it from the circuit and short its leads to discharge it. Connect the ohmmeter across it: the needle first shows a short, then as the capacitor charges from the meter battery it moves toward infinity and ends at an open circuit. A bigger capacitance takes longer. A shorted capacitor stays at low resistance. A leaky dielectric never reaches high resistance but settles at an intermediate value.

Inductor. The common failure is an open. Remove it and test it with the ohmmeter: an open reads infinite resistance, a good coil reads its coil resistance. Overheating may melt the insulation and short turns together, which reduces the effective number of turns. That needs other equipment, not covered here.

4.3.4 · Troubleshooting faults

Opens in a series circuit. Opens occur from overheated resistors or cold solder joints that crack, often after maintenance. With the lamp-and-resistor circuit of Figs 4.35 to 4.37:

  • Voltmeter method: measure the voltage drop across each component. With an open in a series circuit, the full source voltage appears across the open component (Kirchhoff's voltage law) and zero across the good ones. A voltmeter across the good lamp reads 0 V. Across the open resistor, the meter completes the circuit through its very high resistance, so the current is too small to light the lamp, but the meter reads the battery voltage.
  • Ohmmeter method: isolate the part and remove power (open the switch). Across the good lamp you read some resistance. Across the open resistor you read infinity, a discontinuity: the open is found.

Shorts in a series circuit (Figs 4.40 to 4.43). A short is potentially worse than an open: the circuit current is greater than normal. Shorts develop when wire bundles chafe on structure or hydraulic lines, when screws that are too long pierce a bundle, or when wires are pinched under panels. Some are latent or intermittent. Example (Fig 4.42, 100 V): R1 reads 40 Ω, R2 reads 0 Ω, R3 reads 50 Ω, R4 reads 10 Ω. The 0 Ω reading finds the shorted resistor. With a voltmeter, a shorted component shows zero volts and the good ones show some voltage.

Shorts in a parallel circuit (Fig 4.44, 30 V with three 30 Ω resistors). A short in a parallel circuit blows the fuse: a path of almost zero resistance across the battery makes the current rise excessively and the fuse opens almost at once, so there is no time for a current or voltage check. Use an ohmmeter, and as with opens in a parallel circuit, one end of the suspect resistor must be disconnected from the rest of the circuit.

Series-parallel circuits (Figs 4.45 to 4.47).

  1. Open in the series portion: current stops in the whole circuit and the lamp does not light.
  2. Open in the parallel resistor branch: the rest still works. Total resistance rises and total current falls.
  3. Open in the lamp's branch: the circuit still works with more resistance and less current, but the lamp is out.
